# Blooming Rot 2 — The Story

*For the GM only. This is the whole campaign told as one story, beginning to end, in the order it is meant to happen. All the secrets are on the page. Where the table could choose between roads, the story follows the road the design favors, and mentions the others only in passing — the forks and numbers all still live in the reference doc and the phase masters when you need them. Read this the way you'd read a novel. It is one.*

---

## A Note Before the Story

Three plotlines run through this book. You do not need to track them — the story braids them for you — but it helps to know their names. **The money** is the trail of who paid, and it leads to a bank. **The people** is the trail of who knew, and it leads to frightened clerks and grieving families. **The road** is the trail of how things move, and it leads underground. The first two are designed to be solved, and to feel — at the very moment they are solved — like they were never the real question. The third one is the real question. It is the one that goes all the way down to her.

## Prologue — The Hand That Writes

Far below the sunlit world, in the city of Erelhei-Cinlu, a drow noblewoman sits at a desk and writes in the margins of other people's paper.

Her name is Eclavdra, of House Eilserv, and almost no one in this story will ever learn it. She is patient in a way surface people cannot afford to be. Her house intends, one day, to move against the world above — but that day is decades off, and what a slow war needs first is money, cover, and roads. So she has spent years cultivating all three. She found a respectable eastern bank willing to be advised by an anonymous hand whose advice was always, eerily, correct. She found handlers and couriers and brokers, each paid to know only the single link of the chain they touched. And she found something better than any road: an ancient descent beneath the Cairn Hills, older than every kingdom in the story, which a long-dead Aerdy bureaucracy had once surveyed, classified, and — this is the marvelous part — *permitted*. The empire that wrote those permissions is dust. The paperwork still works. She did not build her road to the surface. She rents it from a ghost.

To prove her machine worked, she ran a rehearsal. A blight — the one people came to call the Blooming Rot — destroyed grain on a schedule. Insurance claims were filed *before* the losses they claimed for. Panic was measured, priced, and found acceptable. Recovery was rehearsed. It all worked beautifully, and nobody above ever understood what they had just watched.

Now she is playing for a real prize. The old Overking of the Aerdy east is failing, and by ancient law, whoever would rule after him cannot collect the eastern harbor's revenue until three particular commissioners' seats in the great port of Rel Astra are filled and confirmed. Her instruction to her surface machine fits on a strip of vellum: **harbor before throne.** Empty the three seats — quietly, one death at a time — and then fill them with people the bank owns, before any new crown is seated. Whoever owns those three chairs owns the eastern money the day the succession resolves. Everything else in this story — the man who would not stay dead, the murdered clerk, the missing grain factor, the leak in a good man's office — is scaffolding around that one calendar.

She signs her marginal notes with a single initial. E.

## Chapter One — The Heroes of Loftwick

The story the players live begins on a road, three days out from a battle. Five companions — Alicia, Elle, Gianni, Kitty, and Selvara, with Cam's chair sometimes empty — have done the thing nobody else would do: they went into the Forbidden Orc Keep and came out again. In the ruins they unmasked the fey creature that had worn Mayor Thornwell's face for years — it called itself Kael'thorne — and in its cottage they found old letters signed with a single initial: E. Remember that. It is the first time the initial touches the story, before anyone knows to care. And they brought down a mercenary named **Haskur Vandrell**, who did something no one is supposed to be able to do. He died without leaving a body. A prepared magic snatched his flesh away at the moment of death and left only his belongings behind.

Loftwick receives them as heroes — but watch how carefully. They are brought in by the quiet gate, not the triumphal one, and the man running the welcome is **Caelith Dunivar**, Keeper of Foreign Correspondence: late fifties, scholar's coat, a mind like a filing room. He is not throwing them a parade. He is entering them into the *durable* civic record, naming them Friends of the Common Defense, arming them with sealed tokens that will open doors clerks would rather keep shut. He is protecting them with paperwork, because paperwork is the language of the enemy, though nobody knows that yet.

That night, behind a closed door, he tells them the truth as far as he owns it. Haskur's name has crossed his desk twice, on papers no office ever issued. The man has died before — once on the Onnwal coast, once in a Hardby tavern — and was recovered each time. Somebody underwrites his dying. And in the cellar strongroom, laid in an open chest, are the dead man's effects: a torn spellbook; a wallet of letters; a signet ring bearing a wading bird above three coins; a **letter of credit drawn six weeks before the fight**, annotated *"Hold for V., or his agent of record"* — the grammar of banking closing around a resurrection; a burned ritual focus; a brass map tube whose caravan chart carries a small handwritten "R." beside a roadhouse a day south; and a silver trade token from the far eastern city of Rel Astra. The bird-and-coins mark appears three times among the items. No one names it yet. Caelith asks the five of them to take a private commission: go east, quietly, and find out who paid for Haskur Vandrell's deaths.

The city gives them one soft night — and the story plants its first seeds in it. The cook, Brennan Kepf, is frightened for kin who drive Hardby caravans and have heard the name Vandrell. Somewhere in the building, perhaps, a half-burned page in the midden hints that someone inside Caelith's own office writes letters he never sees. And in the morning, among the ordinary post, comes an envelope sealed with the same bird-and-coins, containing a single line: *"We hope your visit to Loftwick is brief."*

That envelope ends the beginning. The party is already known. Someone in the east does not want them coming. They accept the commission, and Caelith hands them three parting gifts: a letter of introduction to a Hardby notary named **Veska Maelan**, a purse of trade tokens, and one name with no explanation attached — Holyn Sevarian. The story goes east.

## Chapter Two — The Dead Man's Receipts

The next morning the evidence comes up out of the cellar into daylight, laid on numbered linen on the long refectory table — and there is a stranger at that table. **Trina Alvere**, patron of the arts, silver leaf at her collar, tea in hand. "I advise Caelith on people," she says, and it is true as far as it goes. Caelith sets a brass token beside the evidence — a quill above a closed gate — and admits what it means: he is the **Hand of the Duke**, the unwritten part of his office that works beyond the Yeomanry's borders. The commission is real, and so is the danger.

Daylight changes the objects. The burned focus turns out to carry a wax sigil — a three-rayed sun closing into a fist — **that is not in Haskur's handwriting.** Someone else prepared his escape from death. The false travel papers in his wallet were forged *inside* the Yeomanry, on real vellum, over a real retired official's signature: the institutions are already penetrated. The clipped silver coin bears a private countermark, a tower over three waves. Inside the brass map tube nests a second, smaller tube — black lacquer, courier-made, its seal enchanted so that the wrong reader simply *forgets*, its interior wiped, its craftsmanship "elven, but peculiar" in a way no sage in the city can place. Nobody can chase that tube anywhere yet. It is the road plotline, arriving disguised as a curiosity. With it ride a vellum strip reading **HARBOR BEFORE THRONE**, a pouch of sterile show-grain for faking crop claims, and the item that gives the chapter its name: a ledger fragment, three lines, three different hands. *VANDRELL settled. MORAVEN delayed. GILST transferred.* No sums. No dates. Accounting verbs applied to human beings. People carried as accounts — the whole campaign's thesis, six words long, though no one will understand it fully for a very long time.

Down in a hidden records alcove, the names become addresses. The bird-and-coins belongs to **Cindren & Vhal, Bonded Factors** of Rel Astra — a private bank big enough to lend to nations — with a branch in Hardby and a one-clerk satellite office right here, on **Wick Lane**. Merro **Gilst** was a bonds clerk who lodged on that same Wick Lane, above a stationer's; his register entry closed three days ago with a death certified by a contract physician, **Reln Pollow** — also of Wick Lane. Tamsin **Moraven** is a senior grain factor, twelve years in trade, counting house on **Tannerway** — and she has been missing for eleven days. Two streets now hold the whole mystery, and the party spends its days walking them.

Wick Lane is the street of paper and pursuit. At the bank's satellite office, the courteous junior clerk **Hesren Vesh** knows nothing and hides nothing — but shown the dead man's endorsement, his eyes rest on it two breaths too long, and he offers four words that matter: it "reads like Hardby work." The moment the party leaves, he does his honest duty and sends a sealed message to his superiors. That message is the knife starting its travel. In Gilst's sealed rooms above the stationer's, the searcher who came before them missed everything that matters: a hollowed book holding a receipts ledger — four years of quiet payments from "H.V. Hardby," three entries countersigned in a stranger's hand with the initial **E.** — a lozenge tin with a false bottom hiding a signed **bearer note drawn on Cindren & Vhal's Hardby office, in the very hand that endorsed Vandrell's escrow**; a locket with a nameless portrait; and, tucked behind it, a note in the dead clerk's writing: *"If they ask again, I do not know how to keep saying no. If they stop asking, I do not know what that will mean. This is the third winter I have been afraid."* Shown the bearer note, Caelith goes very still. *"We now have the hand,"* he says. *"The hand is in Hardby."* Two doors down, Pollow the physician sweats through an interview and begs two days to leave for Greyhawk; the kind thing and the wise thing are the same thing — let him go, and he becomes a witness who lives.

And the street itself is watching back. A sixteen-year-old former message-runner called **Sparrow** — hungry, paid in coppers by a man she never saw — has been logging every visitor to the stationer's door. Spotted, she bolts across her own ground, and she is faster *here* than anyone in armor will ever be; but vaulting the market wall she loses her oilcloth fold, and inside it the party finds their own descriptions in her tally, the address of a dead-letter drop in the city, and one line of routing: **"east on the Sevenday cart."** Whether they corner her or lose her, the fold is the prize — and what the party does with a scared, hungry girl afterward tells the table who they are. Trina, without being asked, has a back room and a use for someone who knows the lanes.

Tannerway is the street of people. In Tamsin's counting house her chief clerk, **Doril Veth**, red-eyed and precise, opens her employer's last ledger: three insurance claims by Cindren & Vhal's Hardby office on grain shipments, each **filed before the loss it claimed for happened** — by eleven days, by three weeks, by two days. The office's file copies were stolen the day before; blank leaves left in their place. *"That is not theft,"* Caelith says. *"That is being paid to know what tomorrow looks like."* At the Moraven house, Tamsin's husband Vellin — a schoolmaster aged a year in eleven days — hands over the note she left: *"If I am not back by Sevenday, take Ailen out of Hardby."* Ailen is their daughter. Ailen is in Hardby right now. Trina sends a letter to her that same night by a private courier, and the family becomes what the case never was before: urgent.

Then the city squeezes. A polite deputy named **Arthen Moll** calls with drafts for twelve hundred gold apiece and a sincere argument: the party has done enough, and their visible digging is making Yeomanry trade expensive. He is not corrupt. He is the soft wall the establishment puts in front of inquiries. They refuse him — and the night after, the hard answer arrives: **Merev Sarth**, a professional thief on a strict retrieval contract, slips into the Little Palace cellar for the evidence. She finds decoys, because Caelith moved the originals to Trina's townhouse the moment Hesren's courier left; she finds an alarm rune, and a corporal waiting at the far end of her escape tunnel. Taken, she eventually gives up one name — her Hardby broker, a thin, smoker-voiced man called **"Tarsh."** Searched, she gives up something Caelith wanted more: her forged writ carries a counterfeit of the Audit Hall's own seal — the stamp he has been quietly hunting inside his own office for nine months. He does not say the name he suspects. Not for three more chapters.

The choice that ends the chapter is Caelith's folios on the old council table: travel east as an official delegation, with authority and no surprise; travel as merchants under **commercial cover**, with Trina's letter to her old protégé, the Hardby poet Castrian Vell; or ride quiet and fast with nothing but distance. *"I can give you authority, cover, or distance,"* he says. *"I cannot give you all three."* The story follows the middle road, the one he himself would take. At the gate he presses one more thing into the lead rider's hand — a small folded paper: *17 Cooper's Tale, Hardby merchant quarter. Ask Mistress Halver at the corner grocer for the key. Tell her the cellarer's apprentice came down from Loftwick to mind the house.* They read it on the road.

One thread the story leaves deliberately loose: that little "R." on the dead man's map. It leads, if followed, to a roadhouse called the Sign of the Brown Hare and a landlady named **Rosalin Brent** — Haskur's half-sister, who keeps a cedar box of his childhood things and knows nothing of any conspiracy at all. It is the campaign's one honest red herring. It costs days and pays out only humanity. Pay it anyway, once. Not every mark is a door, and the table needs to learn that too.

## Chapter Three — Hardby

Hardby is a merchant free city on the Wild Coast, salty and fast and loose, governed — more in name than in force these days — by an old council of women called the Gynarchy. Its main bell runs five seconds slow and has for three centuries. The conspiracy's working arm lives here.

The party's first act is domestic, and it matters. On a quiet side street they find 17 Cooper's Tale: a shuttered three-storey house under dust sheets. The widowed grocer on the corner, **Mistress Betha Halver**, hands over the key on the right phrase and asks no questions. Under a library floorboard waits a lockbox — maps, sealing wax, an emergency purse — and a letter in red wax addressed *"To whoever finds this."* It is Caelith at his best, and it should be read aloud: the house is theirs; the bank's Hardby branch keeps its house on Counting House Lane, with a bonded clerk named Jaron Pell at the desk who is paid to be civil and to report; the notary Veska Maelan on Whalemarket Quay will be worth more than the bank itself; there is a discreet healer on Cobbler's Walk with twenty gold on account. *"I have not left a weapon in the house. I have left a place to put a weapon down."* And Mistress Halver, kindly treated, turns out to be the merchant quarter's finest intelligence service: the bank clerk Pell is unhappy in his work; a senior partner "who never lets anyone see his hands" has come down from Rel Astra twice in six weeks; a thin smoker-voiced man drinks at the Coopered Wreck with his back to the wall; and Tamsin Moraven herself stepped off a wagon at the eastern caravan yard fifteen days ago, looking tired.

Then comes the scene the whole middle of the campaign stands on. **Veska Maelan**, trade-clearing notary, mid-fifties, Caelith's friend of twelve years, spreads the party's evidence across her table at night and reads it the way other people read faces. The escrow's public seal tells her nothing — anyone can press a house seal. But the Hardby clearing-chop in the margin was applied by **Solen Mereth**, head of the branch's bond-loading office; she knows his hand the way you know your own kitchen in the dark. The marginal endorsement — *Hold for V.* — is a different hand: smaller, older, seen three times in four years. Given a week she will swear to it: it belongs to **Tarlith Vhal Sereth**, senior partner of Cindren & Vhal in Rel Astra, who has not signed a letter at his own desk in eleven years. The firm pretends he is unwell. He is not unwell. He is hiding what he writes. The chipped countermark on the clipped coin? She notarized that stamp as *destroyed* in 579 — and it is stamping paper still, upstairs in the branch strongroom, which makes it perjury you can hold. And then she names the shape of the thing the party cannot see yet. There is a fourth hand. One further annotation, in writing that belongs to no one in Hardby, no one in Rel Astra, no one she can place at all — the hand *the firm answers to*. She can offer only three details about how it forms the letter E: the loop, the serif, the angle of the descender. File those away. They are the first formal observations of the enemy.

The rest of Hardby unfolds from her reading. At the Coopered Wreck, the smoker-voiced man turns out to be **Anver Resh** — "Tarsh" was never a real name — a freelance broker who was once Solen Mereth's clerk. Confronted with paper that names him, he trades everything he knows for a one-way passport: Solen has run a second, secret set of books since 580; Sereth signs what matters; the overseer who runs the rough work is a veteran named **Reyna Worth**; and Tamsin Moraven is alive. What he will not sell at any price is his Rel Astra contact. *"Take what I have given you and go."* Every witness in this story ends at a wall. The walls are the point.

Tamsin is being held at the Whitemoor Estate, a wine-house-turned-residence two days east along the coast, guarded politely by hired blades whose standing order — until the next courier changes it — is *hold safely*. The party goes in by sea at night, ahead of that courier, and brings her out alive: tired, unbroken, and twelve days more valuable than any ledger, because she is the person who can testify to the claims that were filed before their losses. Her daughter Ailen — warned by Trina's letter, guarded by her sculptor's workshop and her own good sense — gets her mother back. (On other roads this chapter ends darker: come as an official delegation and Tamsin is three days dead when you arrive, leaving only a smuggled last letter that will break the table's heart. The story is kinder on this road. Keep the letter anyway; it may still be needed someday.)

Two more doors close out the chapter. In the Gynarchy's registry, an under-clerk named **Zoria Weis** — a debt Caelith is calling in, not a friend — opens the harbor-commission volume and turns the vellum strip's slogan into a calendar. Rel Astra's harbor commission has seven seats; three of them, the First, Third, and Fifth, are filled by old-house appointment that a seated Overking must confirm. All three current holders are dead. Lord Hessar Olm, 582, natural causes. Lady Mirin Vesh, 583, a fall on her own staircase — Worth's work, though no paper says so. Master Ervan Dale, 584, a brief illness that was nothing of the kind. No successors seated. The Aerdy succession is an open question, and whoever has three friendly commissioners waiting when a new Overking is named owns the eastern revenue. *Harbor before throne.* The party now knows the game. They do not yet know the players' names.

And on Counting House Lane, the party takes the bank itself — not with swords but with a junior bond-officer's grief. **Vella Tannin** lost her brother in the coastal troubles of 580, his cargo "disappeared" under a falsified bonded escrow; she has never connected his death to her employer. The party connects it for her, gently, with Veska's help — and for one week she copy-reads the second set of books, eighteen quiet transactions, two of them carrying Sereth's signature pattern. Then they get her out of the city within a day, because the firm kills clerks who talk, and this story does not spend Vella Tannin. With her carbons, Sereth's blotter impression, and Veska's sworn notarial opinion, the party carries out of Hardby the thing the whole first half was building: an indictment package that names a senior partner of Cindren & Vhal in his own hand.

They sail east on a coastal galley called the Lacquer Wave, past a harbor that is starting to look for them. Rel Astra rises out of the morning haze on the third week, and its clocks all strike together.

## Chapter Four — Rel Astra

Rel Astra is the oldest city in the story and the most orderly: Aerdi imperial time, kept exactly. The conspiracy planned its play here, and here the surface half of the story comes to its climax.

The party's guide through it is **Therion Halask**, a retired advocate in his sixties who keeps morning hours at the Brass Sextant coffee-house and answers to a phrase from Caelith's letter: *"By the wheatsheaf and the open scroll."* He speaks in courtly flourishes until the moment matters — when the honorifics stop, listen. He takes one look at the indictment package and takes custody of it, permanently, because he has seen what happens to evidence that travels loose. And he lays out the political board like a tutor with a chessboard. Three empty seats. Three candidates. Three different kinds of capture.

**Lord Faren Mirelth**, the reformer, is *owned*: secretly bankrupt for years, he has taken twenty thousand gold of the bank's money as "personal advances," and the commercial code is blunt about men who sit on commissions while indebted to counting houses with business before them. Document the loans and he is finished — though the kindest end, and the one he would choose, is a quiet conversation and a withdrawal "for personal reasons." **Magister Andrune Vesh**, the scholar, is *leveraged*: an honest man whose widowed sister-in-law **Lyla** faces twenty years for her late husband's customs forgeries, with the bank's fixers murmuring that the case could vanish if he takes his seat. Nothing disqualifies him. The only clean way to free him is to free *her* — and Halask, given two weeks and a modest purse, can demolish the case on precedent alone. Pay it. It buys an honest man's soul back at filing-fee prices. **Mistress Coriath Tenrel**, the advocate, is *willing*: no debts, no hostages — she came to Sereth herself with an offer, and the only way to stop a volunteer is to stop the seating itself. Between them, the three candidates teach the party to read capture anywhere, for the rest of their lives.

Around that spine the chapter breathes. **Councilor Belven Astor**, an old mariner turned harbor-ward councilor, becomes their one civic ally, providing statements and warning them which colleagues not to trust. In the imperial registry they find the campaign's fourth observation of the enemy's hand: on the deferral that has held the Third Seat open for fourteen months, a marginal note in that same unplaceable writing — *"Sufficient. Hold open until the second autumn after the next."* The hand does not just fund the conspiracy. It sets the conspiracy's calendar, in writing, years in advance, under a hard dark wax that matches nothing in the empire. And when the party's progress begins to hurt, the enemy answers the only way it has left: fire. On a night the design telegraphs in advance, hired men come for Astor's chandlery and its records. The party is meant to be there — one honest brawl in a chapter of paper, and a captured arsonist whose trail leads back to a bank-aligned junior advocate who has been quietly seeding rumors against Halask. There is also, if they want it, a stranger audience: **Lord Galenix Naelax**, the Aerdy claimant himself, under polite house-arrest — a decent, bookish man who owes the bank thirty-eight thousand gold and has never met a single conspirator, who does not yet understand that his debt has already been called, and whose whole role in the enemy's plan is to be crowned and then collected. Tell him the truth honestly and he becomes the play's undoing from the inside; the story leaves that door ajar.

Then the confrontation. The bank's building on the Old Harbor Ward, brass plate, four storeys. **Tarlith Vhal Sereth** receives them not in the grand audience room but in his private working office, where the light falls on the visitors and not on him — which is his way of saying he considers them a serious problem. He is late fifties, thin, silver-bearded, exact; he never raises his voice, never threatens, and smiles only with his mouth. They lay the package before him, and he responds like what he is: a senior operator handling an unexpected procedural problem. He offers the quiet ending — his resignation, his candidates withdrawn, papers surrendered — and when they ask him, directly, about the annotations in the margins of his firm's decisions, he tells them the truth, because the truth costs him nothing and unsettles them more than any lie: *"The annotations are not from this side of the great desert. I have not met the writer. I do not know whether the writer is a person, an office, or a guild. I have, perhaps once a year, received a note in that hand, and the firm has acted on what was in the note. The notes were always correct. I do not ask further."* The named villain of the first act, confessing that he too reports upward into a void. The party files anyway. Halask's indictment goes to the commercial court; the honest magistrate refuses every motion to dismiss; the harbor commission suspends its candidate votes pending trial; and ninety days after the party leaves the city, Tarlith Vhal Sereth is convicted, stripped of his estates, and barred from commercial office for twenty years. Mirelth withdraws. Vesh withdraws free. Tenrel, with no seating left to join, files her ambition away for a better decade. The harbor play collapses for at least two years.

It is a complete victory, and the story makes sure it tastes like one — right up until Caelith's letters catch up with them. One says that Trina has sent a copy of Veska's opinion to someone she calls only *"my old correspondent in the wood."* One says, with terrible calm: *"The clerk in my office is named Ostren Pell. I have known this for nineteen days. I have done nothing about it."* One reports Haskur Vandrell seen alive in Greyhawk City, at the door of a counting-house called the Brass Crow, in company with a younger man whose gait was "not Aerdi and not Yeomanry." And the last one contains the sentence the whole campaign turns on, the one a player will someday quote back across the table: *"The conspiracy you have named is not the conspiracy."*

They ride west with a conviction in their saddlebags and a question they cannot put down.

## Chapter Five — The Small Matter

The Loftwick they come home to has been rearranged in their absence. The council's closure faction won its vote while they were away; the Vandrell matter is officially *resolved*; Caelith's office is nine days from being voted out of existence, and the brass service pin is gone from his lapel. He meets them behind a barred door and lays four papers on the desk, one for each moving piece of the quiet last act.

The first is the closure motion and its clock: a binding vote on the ninth day. The second is the leak. *"His name is Ostren Pell."* A young correspondence clerk, recruited years ago through his dead father's gambling debt — and locked in place by a second envelope that contained nothing but his sister's name. **Sera Pell** is a dressmaker, forty-one, sharp-eyed, entirely innocent, entirely unaware — and she keeps her shop on Wick Lane, the same narrow street where Merro Gilst lived and died, because the conspiracy recruits where it watches. Caelith has known for three weeks and done nothing, because removing Pell tells the enemy he knows, and because the man who pays Pell is holding Sera's safety like a coin. He hands the party the decision he cannot make cleanly himself, and he prices every option without steering: expose Pell and burn the channel; turn him and take on the daily work of guarding his sister; feed the channel careful lies; shadow it silently upward; or end it. The story takes the patient road — shadow first, then turn. Pell, confronted with kindness instead of ruin, confesses in one continuous breath and then starts negotiating for Sera three sentences in, and both instincts are real. From then on the party runs him together: his packets say what Caelith wants said, and two of the five are never far from a dressmaker's shop that has no idea it is the most protected address in the city.

The third paper is the sealed reply from the wood. Trina's mysterious correspondent turns out to be **Aldea Veren**, a half-elven retired paleographer living a careful day's ride west with a dog, two goats, and an owl on the workshop roof — a woman who reads *matter* the way Veska reads hands. Her findings, read aloud in the strongroom, are the first hard proof that the campaign's compass has been pointing the wrong direction. The dark wax on the enemy's seals matches no surface recipe she has ever documented, anywhere. The handwriting is court-trained but not court-*raised* — someone taught themselves the surface's official hand from documents, without ever sitting in a room where it was used; the strokes are built in the wrong order. And the oldest dated annotation is a geographic impossibility: no living person could have traveled from any port in the world to where that page was signed in the time the dates allow. She will not write down what she thinks it means. But if someone visits her cottage and asks precisely the right narrow question — *could the writer have learned this hand from someone who has never been to the surface?* — she will look at them for a long moment and not say no. East was never the answer. The answer is *down*. Nobody says the word yet.

The fourth paper is the Greyhawk affidavit — Haskur at the Brass Crow — and it becomes the compass needle. But first the courier chain has to be walked, and it is one of the story's quiet pleasures: a loose brick in the civic copy room; a pipe-smoking courier named Trell who crosses Cooper's lane on Tuesday and Friday mornings; a rented room above a printer on Quill Street, let to a soft-spoken "northern lawyer" who signs himself **V. Quill**; a lead pipe behind the Hall of Bonds with a receipt inside, initialed simply "V." Taken quietly on the eighth day, Vesten Quill gives up the one thing he has: the packets go north, to a letter agency in Greyhawk City called Kestrel & Reed. He does not know what stands above it. Nobody in this story ever knows what stands above them. That is how she built it.

The ninth day, the party fights its last Loftwick battle in a council chamber. The co-sponsor of the closure motion, a widowed councilor named Halvern, only ever wanted the budget for her southern militia — so the party finds her militia its five thousand gold from private hands, Trina putting up most of it, the deal presented properly by allies rather than by strangers. A procedural ally, Councilor Voss, re-opens the vote with the courier receipt as evidence that the "resolved" matter has been reading the council's own mail. The motion fails by one vote. The brass pin comes back to Caelith's lapel — he had it in his coat pocket the whole time, waiting. And on the morning the party rides out, he hands them a sealed letter to be read a day down the road, and says the line that ends the act: *"We have more doors than hands. Choose your door."*

They choose Greyhawk. The story was always going to choose Greyhawk.

## Chapter Six — The Brass Crow

Greyhawk City is too large to care whether anyone is right, and money is the only language spoken without an accent. Somewhere in its Foreign Quarter, in a counting-house with a brass crow over the lintel, the man the party killed before this story began is registered under the name **H. V. Andren**.

The chapter is a hunt, and its hunters are gathered one by one. A lame old Yeomanry sergeant, **Tellan Verth**, drinking every night at the Hand of Coals beside the chimney, who saw Haskur enter the Brass Crow with a young man in grey-and-bone clothing — a young man who watched the door lintel and the reflections in windows instead of the people, and whose walk was made of small constant corrections, *"like he was used to walking somewhere narrower."* A watch kept from a cobbler's upper room across the way, noting who arrives and what they wait for, because Caelith's note said not to walk in on the first day and Caelith is always right about days. In the registry ledger, beside Andren's name and no other client's, a tiny side-mark: **a vertical stroke crossed at three-quarter height.** File that mark away with the loop and the serif and the descender. It is a courier's mark, and the party will see it again, far below the world, on every registry they ever read. A manuscript dealer in the Garden Quarter, **Iren Velash**, who stops blinking when she is interested and who — given everything at once, spread on her reading table — produces the reading the phase is named for. And a fastidious little archive-seller on the High Quay, **Edrik Vone**, whose irregular ledger, bought at an irregular price, shows monthly packets flowing from the letter agency Kestrel & Reed to a rotating recipient: *V. K. — V. Korre — K—*, current address a room above a bakery on Cordwainer's Row, and one pickup pending: *eleventh bell, two nights from now, old bath, Tinker's Way side.*

**Veyra Korre** is the enemy's Greyhawk handler — late thirties, careful, loyal to nothing but her own tradecraft — and the campaign's honest tragedy is that she is nobody. She moves sealed correspondence for pay. She has never met her principal. *"The kind of person who can afford that kind of wax,"* she says, if cornered kindly, *"does not write their name."* At the letter agency, a senior clerk named Doras Kemmel — a Hardby man with a wife and three children — turns witness the moment somebody offers his family real protection, and his lockbox wall shows the party their own story from underneath: Quill's Loftwick packets came through here; summaries of Caelith's office reached Korre monthly; and box seventeen is one packet light this week, collected by *"a young man in grey-and-bone clothing."*

Everything converges at the eleventh bell, in the **Old Stonecistern** under a derelict bathhouse — a great vaulted dark of standing water and old tile, lamplight flickering down through a street grate, a lit brazier waiting at the platform's end. Above the low iron door, scratched fresh in the soot, a mark: seven descending cuts beneath a black hill. This is not the underworld. It is only city plumbing. But it is a rehearsal for a descent, and everyone in the room except the party knows it. Haskur is brought in — alive, diminished, and not in charge of anything, a package being handed over. The young man, **Kethren Ilvath**, comes in from the far drain to receive him. Korre's people stand ready to burn whatever cannot travel. What follows can be an ambush, a shadowing, a parley, or a scramble — and the design is generous, because whatever happens, three things survive the night: the soot-fresh route mark on the wall; a scorched or captured itinerary bearing two lines the party will never stop turning over — *"No sail. No road. VII Stair."* and *"Blooming was proof. Rot was proof. Recovery was the question."* — and the shape of the truth. Korre is the person the conspiracy can afford to lose. Kethren is the person it cannot. And Haskur — the villain of the wilderness, the man who would not stay dead — says the line that reorders the whole story, if anyone is there to hear it: *"I was not rescued. I was collected."*

In the morning, an unsigned note in a familiar hand reaches their lodgings: *"The city was the desk. You have found the drawer."*

## Chapter Seven — The Seventh Question

The party leaves the cistern holding a phrase and a sketch, and spends this chapter turning them into a place on a map that says the place is not there.

Five interpreters, none of whom holds the whole answer — because the enemy compartmentalizes, the story does too, and the players solve it the way you solve anything real: by cross-reference. Iren Velash, reading since the third bell with the itinerary pinned under river-stones, establishes that *Seventh Stair* is not sailors' language — in the oldest route-glossaries, VII marks a *class of permitted descent* under obsolete Aerdy mining law, and "no sail, no road" is not poetry but a transit condition. Aldea Veren, receiving wax and stone-dust by courier, reads the material: mine-seal wax from sealed claims; iron-grey stone with a thread of green, southern Cairn Hills; and the "black hill" in the mark is no hill at all — it is an old surveyor's convention for a **sealed entrance**. Edrik Vone, more frightened and more expensive than last month, sells the route-exception ledger where the word STAIR only ever appears beside the account prefix VII, destination: **Tarnsmere Toll** — and his own unsent note shows he was one step from following it himself. At the Guild of Miners and Jewelers, an old claim sheet gives up its secrets crookedly: claim VII/0341, the claimant's name physically scraped off the page, the Survey's seal re-waxed *seven years after the Survey was dissolved*, and, in the margins of the file, the name of a Yeomanry surveyor — **Garrold Eft** — who rode out to survey the Tarnsmere spur in 542 and never came back. And a retired surveyor named **Dalmor Vetch**, two fingers short and no patience for clean maps, draws them the way in over small beer: the old eastern trade route, the overgrown spur, the dry ford, the split ash, the ruined tollhouse, the cairn. *"Miners call it a stair,"* he says, *"when the earth charges first."* At the bottom of his sketch: *"If the seventh cut is gone, you are too late, or you are at the wrong cairn."*

The enemy tries once to hand them a forgery — a helpful map to a stair with *six* cuts and a single oak, on parchment far too clean — and even swallowed whole it only proves the real marker by exclusion. Two days' ride northeast of the city, where the ruts vanish into grass and a waymark's seventh cut has been scraped within the week, the party crests a rise at sunset and finds it: a roofless tollhouse, a collapsed survey station, and a chest-high cairn whose east face carries, in the old soot of a dozen winters, **seven descending lines beneath a hill that is not a hill.** The rubbing they take matches the cistern sketch exactly. Hand it across the table and let the click happen — because this is the moment each of the five realizes the place is somehow *theirs*. Gianni has known since the old imperial maps showed seven mining spurs where the new maps show three: Tarnsmere is one of the missing four. Elle's order has a key and a sealed record and a prayer for "those who descended" that nobody could ever explain. Selvara has been dreaming this staircase since the first night of the campaign, always waking on the seventh step. Alicia's blade hums faintly, and her pact-mark has gone cold. And Kitty — when she touches the seventh cut, the cut is faintly warm. There has not been a city bell since the road ran out. The silence is not silence. It is administered.

They make a cold camp above the tollhouse, and behind its ruined inner wall waits a stepped, iron-bound door sealed with old wax. Nobody opens it tonight.

## Chapter Eight — The Permission Door

Everything about Tarnsmere is backwards, and each backwards thing teaches the same lesson. The toll window faces *downhill* — the tollhouse was never watching for travelers coming up the road; it was watching for people coming back *up out of the ground*. Behind a long-gone coat rack, seventeen small tally cuts are scratched into the plaster, one for every admitted descent. The bottom three are recent. In a registry fragment in the cellar rooms, old admissions are recorded under the phrase the party has been chasing for two chapters — *"No sail. No road. Toll paid beneath."* — and several entries end with a small, terrible bookkeeping note: **"Return: nil."** Two of those entries are halfling cousins-of-record, names half-burned, and Elle — carrying the list her network friend Hesp compiled of thirty-one halflings who took eastbound contracts and never came home — reads the page and does not speak for a while.

In a collapsed side passage they find the man who tried to tell everyone, eighty years too early. Garrold Eft, surveyor, dead under a roof-fall since 542, his satchel intact, his pocket journal legible. His last pages are the chapter's sermon: *"The site is not a tollhouse. It is a witness-post… The Aerdy did not make the descent. The Aerdy permitted the descent. They were not the first. They are not the last."* He wrote that he heard footsteps on the lower stair, twice, going *up* — and turning aside before they reached the landing. The party should read Eft before they face the door. It is what permits them to feel small in front of it.

The door itself, at the bottom of the hidden stair, is the campaign's argument carved in stone. A flush slab, seven cuts at chest height, the blackened hill, a coin-sized socket, four lines of instruction: *Show token. Speak condition. Lay tally. Witness given.* No glow. No riddle of souls. **Paperwork.** Someone, very long ago, wrote a procedure for walking beneath the world, and the procedure still works — that is the horror, and it is also the puzzle, and the table solves it with the things the story has been quietly handing them: a descent token from a crevice in the cairn, the registry phrase from the fragment, six smooth stones for the tally. And there is a seventh way, carried unknowing through half the campaign. Elle's order gave her an iron key in trust — the relic of that 374 expedition, passed down through six carriers who never knew what it was. Its brass-and-tin ring is the size and shape of a Tarnsmere token. At the door, for the first time in its long life, the ring grows warm. It unscrews. Ask her player, quietly, whether she uses it. Either answer is character.

The slab rises without a sound. Beyond it, a long gallery of stairs descends by seven landings, stone benches set every twenty feet as if for resting porters, the masonry going stranger and older as it goes down. Ten paces in, the party notices that echoes from above have stopped arriving. By the fourth landing, Alicia's pact-mark is not silent the way a closed door is silent — it is silent the way a sky is empty. Whatever her patron is, it cannot reach her here. Whatever notices her next will not be her patron. At the bottom, a wider chamber, and a passage broad enough for four abreast breathing air that is not cold, only *different*. Set into the lintel, in a script older than everything above, the words the campaign has been walking toward since a breakfast envelope in Loftwick:

*This place was opened in the year that the road was first paid for. This place was already old. It is not for those who arrive. It is for those who come back.*

Above them, the world keeps its accounts. Below, something has been keeping theirs.

## Chapter Nine — Below the Clean Paper

The eighth landing opens into **Witness Station Seven**, and the party finally stands in the basement of every ledger they have ever read.

It is a waystation, not a fortress: an account hall of seven stone counter-bays with token slots and tally columns and nothing soft anywhere; a token cage; a route office; sleeping alcoves where an earlier captive scratched *not witnessed… not witnessed…* in tiny prayer-script; a supply cache; a burn furnace with a basket of surface paper queued for the flame. At the third counter-bay, an inscription does what the whole campaign promised. *"H.V.A. — Recovered cargo. Escort: K. Witness pending. Recoverable but unreliable."* Haskur Vandrell, the villain of the wilderness, the man whose death began the story, filed as freight with a courier assigned. *Settled, delayed, transferred* — the ledger fragment from the refectory table, all those chapters ago — was never metaphor. In the route office, Korre's signed surface directive sits beside the route clerk's shorthand of it: same content, two languages, one obligation — the cleanest single proof in the story that respectable surface institutions feed the road. On a crate of blighted grain samples hangs a stone tag: *"Claim precedes loss: yes. Surface panic acceptable: yes."* — the claims Doril Veth wept over in a Loftwick counting house were priced down here first. And rescued from the furnace basket, a slip with *Andren* on one side and, on the other, in a hand that matches no clerk in residence: *"Blooming was proof. Rot was proof. Recovery was the question."* Somewhere below, the road employs someone with a conscience. Keep that hand. It is a friend the story has not met yet.

The station's warden is a pale, gloved, ageless clerk called **Savax**, and he is not a villain; he is a believer in procedure who offers correction before force, because his standing instructions say the route's default is to *classify* trespassers, not kill them. Those same instructions contain the campaign's final, driest revelation, item six: *"Defer all questions of higher authority to channel beyond Black Ledger Gate. The route's deeper figures will respond to formally filed queries; do not pursue informally."* There it is, complete. You cannot knock on Eclavdra's door. You cannot fight your way to her. **You have to file.** In a sealed cell they find **Merrit Osk**, a Greyhawk clerk three weeks held, interviewed by a man in grey-and-bone whose face she cannot remember; her written statement, if they carry her out, ends with her mother's address in the Free Quarter, and it is the phase's whole heart in two lines. Above the Andren column, and again on the crate tag, sits one more mark the party has never seen before: **a shallow arc with three notches.** They will not learn whose it is for a long time. You know whose it is.

At the far end waits the **Black Ledger Gate** — dark stone, no handle on this side, one scraped inscription: *"Unwitnessed cargo waits. Witnessed passage continues."* It does not check who you are. It checks *what you have declared yourself to be* — witness, escort, cargo — and the party, who began this story as heroes of a grateful city, must now pick a category and speak it to a door. Beyond the gate, a chamber where three corridors lead away under three marks: the recovery route, where Haskur went; the escort route, which is Kethren's; the supply route, which fed the Blooming Rot. And turning back — climbing all the way up to the sun with their proof and their rescued witness and their terrible new understanding — is an honored ending too, because Caelith told them once that proof only has weight if someone survives to use it.

There is no boss fight at the bottom of this book. There is a fork in an old road, and a filing procedure, and far below it all, a patient woman in a lightless city who has still never once been named out loud — who signs herself with a single letter, and who has just noticed that five names from the surface have acquired a category of their own.

That is where Part Two of the saga will someday begin.

## Epilogue — Where the Story Stands Tonight

At your table, the story has reached the middle of Chapter Two. The Keep is behind them; the letters signed E. are in their pack; the daylight review and the records alcove are done; the first investigation day is spent — the gaol where Sara Thimblecut pointed at a name and was poisoned before she could speak it, the library where a dead clerk's card wore a bird-and-coins mark, the alcove where the mark became the name Cindren & Vhal, the counting house where the claims arrived before their losses. Five of them stood at the mouth of Wick Lane at nightfall, three doors in front of them.

What comes next is the middle of this chapter: the dead man's rooms above the stationer's, the tin with the false bottom, the fear note, a girl called Sparrow who runs, a polite man with drafts for twelve hundred gold, a thief in the cellar, and three folios on an old council table. The roadhouse with the "R." is out there too, patiently being nothing but a sister and a cedar box. And somewhere under everything, a hand is writing in the margins, six chapters ahead of them, waiting to be read.
