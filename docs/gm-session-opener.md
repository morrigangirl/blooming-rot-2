# Blooming Rot 2 — The Session Opener

*A spoken recap monologue for the return session. Second person, you to the party. Everything in it is information the table already holds — nothing new is revealed except two questions, planted where marked, aimed at the suppositions they haven't said out loud yet (the ledger inversion, and the reach into the gaol). Player quotes are verbatim-close from the transcript; deliver them as callbacks. Bracketed lines are stage directions — don't read them. Time marks assume 160 words per minute. Spoken text runs about 4,700 words: thirty minutes at your pace, with the marked pauses.*

---

**[~0:00]**

Settle in. It has been a while since we sat at this table, and the five of you have been standing in the same evening light for two months — so before anyone rolls anything, I'm going to give you back what you already have. Don't take notes yet. Just listen. Everything I'm about to say, you found. Every conclusion I'm about to repeat, one of you said out loud, and I wrote it down when you did. This is not me telling you a story. This is me reading your own case back to you.

Here is where it starts.

You went into the Forbidden Orc Keep when no one else in the Yeomanry would go. Let's be honest about how you did it, too — you didn't carve your way through a horde of orcs and goblins and worse. You painted yourselves. You walked through them, in war-paint, on nerve and wit and materials supplied on demand by Alicia's remarkable book, and the horde let you pass because you looked like you belonged to it. Steel came later. Steel always comes later with this group. I want you to remember the feel of that day for a second — the noise of that horde all around you, the smell of the paint drying on your skin, the long walk through a crowd of things that would have killed you if any one of them had looked twice. None of them looked twice. You didn't sneak past that horde and you didn't fight through it. You out-thought it. That is the group I'm talking to tonight, and I need you to remember that about yourselves, because everything that has happened since has been that same walk in different clothes.

In the ruins beyond, you ended two things.

The first wore the face of Mayor Thornwell — had worn it for years — and underneath the face it was a creature of the fey that called itself Kael'thorne. Kitty. You knew her twice over. She was the thing at the end of the ruins, and she was — your words — "the person who brought Kitty back." You put her down anyway. And in her cottage, afterward, you found letters. Old letters, kept, and every one of them signed with a single initial.

E.

Hold on to that. You're going to hear it again.

The second thing you ended was a man. Haskur Vandrell. Mercenary, traveler, wizard of real power — Selvara, you clocked that from the spell components alone: "they're a powerful spellcaster," you said, and you were right. You brought him down, all of you together — and here is the part that this entire investigation grew out of, so I'll say it slowly.

He did not have the decency to leave a body.

At the moment of his death, a prepared magic took his flesh and vanished it away, and left his belongings sitting on the ground like a suit of clothes with no man in them. Selvara, when you looked back on that moment, you shrugged and named it in four words: "They zapped themselves away, didn't they? Contingency." Yes. They did. And Kitty — you did the real work on it later, in daylight, with the man's own gear on the table. A very powerful spell had been channeled through his arcane focus. A contingency. And the pages of his spellbook that described it had been torn out — deliberately — *after* it was used.

Somebody built that man an escape hatch out of death itself. And you have since learned it wasn't the first time. He'd died before. Once in a bar fight. Once by an apparent drowning. And each time, somebody pulled him back out.

Now — Gianni. Weeks ago, thinking out loud about those torn pages, you said something I want everyone to hear again, because you weren't guessing, you were deducing. You said: maybe the person who ripped these pages out is "the person that is *funding* the contingency spell." That they keep the pages as — and I'm quoting you — "my hold on you. You'll do what I say if you want these pages back."

Funding. You said funding. Remember that word. A man does not buy that kind of dying with his own purse.

**[~5:00]**

Three days' ride brought you home to Loftwick, and Loftwick did something cities almost never do for adventurers. It thanked you properly. Not with a parade — with paperwork. You were named Friends of the Common Defense. Your names went into the durable record, the one that doesn't get thrown out with the season's news. And each of you was given a sealed wooden token — you've all felt the weight of it by now — the little object that makes clerks sigh, and archivists move, and gaolers grudgingly open doors. Elle, you have already field-tested exactly how far that token goes, and exactly where it stops — it will make a gaol guard jump, and it will not make a gaol door open. Institutions have layers. You've been learning which layers answer to gratitude, and which ones answer to something else. We'll get there.

And then there was Caelith Dunivar. Keeper of Foreign Correspondence. Grey-haired, scholar's coat, a mind like a filing room with every drawer labeled. He welcomed you in public with full honors — the steps, the scribes, the register — and then he closed a door, and told you the truth. And notice, in hindsight, the shape of what he gave you first. Not gold. Not titles that make speeches. Tokens and records — instruments. The man thanked you in the only currency this enemy respects, before any of you knew what the enemy was. That should have told you something about him, and I think, quietly, it did.

He is the Hand of the Duke. The title appears on no paper, and it must continue to appear on none, because the Duke is in trouble. You've seen the leaflets in the streets — a manufactured scandal about grain, accusations Caelith calls faceless and baseless, and the Council circling. Caelith himself is watched everywhere he goes. He cannot investigate the dead man's money through his own office.

And then he told you the worse thing. He isn't certain his office is *secure*. His correspondence — think about how strange this sentence is — his correspondence has been giving answers to questions that were never asked.

Kitty. You didn't let that slide past. You said it back to him, in your own words, plainer than he'd dared to say it himself: "I think somebody is using your office's network — without your knowledge."

He agreed. Someone inside his own walls is using the Duke's resources. Which means: nothing entrusted to the palace is entrusted at all. You understood that instantly, and you've already acted on it once — but I'm getting ahead of myself.

So Caelith gave you the commission. Private. Unofficial. Off every book in the city. Find out who underwrote Haskur Vandrell's deaths. And he joined one person to your number: Trina Alvere. Patron of the arts. His advisor — as she puts it — "on people." A woman who can make an entire town's gossip sit up and testify. A particular friend of Alicia's. I'm saying nothing further about the arm-in-arm walking. The record notes it. The record declines to interpret it.

[glance at Alicia's player; move on before she recovers]

**[~8:00]**

Now. The evidence. The dead man's effects, laid out on numbered linen in the Little Palace, examined in daylight by your own hands. I'm going to walk the table one item at a time, and as I do, listen to how much of this *you* cracked.

The burned spell focus. Kitty established the contingency was channeled through it. But the focus had two more things to say. In the melted wax at its end: a sigil, resembling an ancient, wrong-shaped Elvish letter E. And pressed into that wax — Gianni found it, because of course Gianni found it — a partial fingerprint. Someone's actual finger touched that seal. Not Haskur's sigil. Not Haskur's hand.

The spellbook. Half its leaves torn out in one hurried pull — Gianni, you established they came out all at once, in a hurry, nearly destroying the binding. What remained: a comprehend-languages spell, some obscuring spell, and the stub of a divination so complex Selvara couldn't place it — a working that wanted, of all things, a hummingbird's feather. And the table did something with that detail I have honestly never forgotten. Gianni: "hummingbirds — they hover, they watch, they dart. It'd be basically a little spy drone." Kitty: "they're pollinators — it might be something to do with *predicting the harvest*." Watching. Predicting. Harvests. File that. You built that theory out of a feather, and you had no idea yet how right you were going to look.

And on a surviving scrap, in a script that apes Elvish without being Elvish, a line that Alicia's magic dragged into the light: "You should fear your master more than your enemy. This is your last chance. Fail me again and suffer the consequences."

Signed: E.

The same E as the wax on the focus. The same E that signed the letters in a dead fey's cottage. Kitty, you strung those beads the moment the note was read: "Signed E — and there was an E on the focus, and the letter was signed E as well." One correspondent. Three appearances. No face. And notice what kind of letter it is. That's not a rival writing. That is an employer writing. A performance review, from someone a man who could cheat death was afraid of.

The false letter of passage. For one "Haskur Voren, agricultural assessor." Alicia, you called the alias the second you saw it. And the letter is the quietest frightening thing on the whole table, because everything about it is real. Real Yeomanry vellum. A real retired official's genuine signature. A forgery manufactured with authentic materials, from inside the machine — undone by one detail: crop assessors are posted. They do not travel. Kitty, your instinct on the spot was to go find that retired official and ask him some questions. That instinct was correct. It is still correct.

The letter of credit. Ten thousand gold. Kitty read it: earmarked for ship, crew, victuals, passage — not pocket money, *logistics* — drawn six weeks before the Keep fell, and annotated in a margin: "Hold for V., or his agent of record."

Six weeks before. Somebody knew his timeline before you did. Somebody had already paid for the pickup.

And I'll say this, because it belongs in the record too: you even ran down the dead ends properly. Kitty spent a good ten minutes trying to determine whether the hand that wrote in that book was left or right — got nowhere, said so, and moved on. That's not wasted time. That's what thoroughness looks like from the inside: most of it doesn't pay, and you do it anyway, and then the one check in ten breaks a case open.

The signet ring, worn daily — not kept in a drawer as a keepsake, *worn*, the way you wear something you use — and a sealed letter under the same device: a long-legged wading bird, call it crane or heron as you please, above three coins. The mark of a private banking house of the east. The clipped coin that isn't a coin at all — a Rel Astran accounting token, a receipt pretending to be money, struck with a private exchange-mark. Selvara, while the rest of the table was still sorting objects, you stood up and said the thesis: "They've got a really good cover tied to some sort of *banking*. There's a lot of money flowing." That was you, weeks before you had a name to put on it. You'll hear the name again in a minute — because you went and *got* the name.

**[~13:00]**

The black-lacquered tube. Courier-work of curious and foreign make. Alicia identified the seal's enchantment — break it wrong, and you simply *forget*. The five of you can personally testify to how well that works. Inside: routes. Sea routes, trade routes, caravan routes — from Hardby, to Loftwick. And beside a roadhouse a day south of the city, in the dead man's own hand, a single letter: R. Meaning, as of tonight: still unknown.

Think about what a tube like that is *for*. It isn't a container. A container wants its contents kept. This thing wants its contents deniable — a message that rides hidden inside another message, that burns its own tracks, that makes the wrong reader forget the reading. Whoever equips their couriers like that has been moving secrets professionally for a long, long time, along those exact roads: Hardby to Loftwick. Somebody drew your city at the *end* of a supply line.

The vellum strip. Four words in careful capitals: HARBOR BEFORE THRONE. An old imperial-sounding phrase — the kind of words that come from somewhere with harbors worth fighting over and thrones worth waiting for — and a phrase that keeps company, on this table, with the east.

The seed pouch — sewn secretly into the spellbook's binding. Sterile durum wheat, and a slip reading "Seed for the dry barony." Kitty, you named the scam on sight: show-grain for false crop claims. Proof of origin that can never be planted to prove anything. And then the whole table lit up at once. Gianni, walking through what sterile seed does to a supply chain: harvests fail, reputations fail, "you're going to essentially, in a couple of years, starve out their population" — and then the line I have been waiting two months to read back to you. Gianni Varren, on the record:

"We're not just looking at sabotage here. We're looking at profit. Crop failures mean loans. Loans mean debt. Debt means control — and someone's using the Duke's own agricultural system to bleed them dry."

And Selvara, quietly, right behind it — dependence on whom? Your words: "people that arrive from the East, for instance."

Loans. Debt. Control. The East. You two built the entire engine of this conspiracy out of a bag of dead wheat, and you did it before lunch.

Which brings us to the last item. The ledger fragment. Three lines, three different hands, the middle one in blue ink:

VANDRELL settled. MORAVEN delayed. GILST transferred.

You worked this one hard. Alicia read it as debts — "an outstanding debt… you're settled." Kitty ran with it — "he's paid his debt, somebody else has delayed their payment, and someone else — the debt's been bought out." And then, Alicia, you noticed the thing that a professional investigator gets paid to notice. You squinted at it and said: "it was described as a ledger… a ledger would normally have *numbers*, you'd think."

[pause. let that sit.]

A ledger with no numbers. No sums. No dates. No totals. Three lines of accounting language — and the only entries on the page are *names*.

So here is the first question I'm going to leave sitting in the middle of the table tonight, and I want it to itch: if it is a ledger — and it is — and there is no money on it — and there isn't — then what, exactly, is being counted?

[do not answer it. move on.]

**[~17:00]**

That was the table. Then you did what real investigators do. You split the city between you, and you went to work. One day. Four threads. Watch what you brought back.

Elle. You went to the gaol, alone, to see Sara Thimblecut — an old acquaintance, a halfling of flexible profession, lately jailed, and looking sickly even as you sat down. Picture the room again. Stone alcoves. A table built into the wall. Bars between you and her, set back so far that even a tall man couldn't reach through. Two guards at the door behind you, one inside — flipping through a little notebook, not really paying attention. Everything about that room is designed so that nothing can reach a prisoner. Hold that thought. She looked at your list of names. She leaned in. She said — and this is exact — "No, I… well, this one here." She pointed. And before the name could leave her mouth, she was coughing. Then gagging. Then green-faced and foaming, convulsing on the far side of the bars, and the guards did *nothing* until you put your token of esteem in their faces and made them move. She went to the infirmary. Whether she lives — you still don't know. What name her finger was on — you still don't know.

But Elle — you knew what you had just watched. You rolled brilliantly, and you said it: the timing was "awfully convenient." Somebody didn't want her to talk. And Kitty, from the symptoms alone, you named the poison — foxglove, and a lot of it.

Now think about what that means, and I'm going to say it as a question, because it's the second one I want itching at you tonight. Sara Thimblecut was inside a locked gaol, behind bars, behind guards, behind stone. She started to say a name to you — only to you — and she was silenced *between one word and the next*. So: who can reach a woman inside a locked cell that fast? And how did they know to be ready — before you ever knocked?

You already decided, that same afternoon, not to file for palace permission to visit her again — because petitions filed at the palace pass beneath the leak's eye. Nobody argued. You all just… knew. That's not paranoia. That's pattern recognition. Sit with what the pattern is telling you.

[breath. shift register — warmer.]

Kitty and Gianni. The Grand Library. Lorien Dunivar — the man who once drew an archfey's hooks out of Kitty's cheek; the silver filaments are still there — took you down to the Civic Register, where by the Duke's order every visitor to this city is carded and kept. And you two went digging, drawer by drawer, and came up with treasure.

One: Merro Gilst. The third name on the fragment. A bonds clerk. Rooms on Wick Lane, at the Shambord House. His entry: closed. His status: recorded dead — certified by one Reln Pollow, a contract physician. And on Gilst's card, stamped small and neat: the bird and three coins. The bank's own mark, on a dead clerk's registry card.

Two: one "Haskur Voren" — the false letter's alias — lodged in this city for a single night, six weeks ago. Kitty, you caught the echo before I finished the sentence: "Six weeks ago — that's…" Yes. That's the season the letter of credit was drawn. The dead man walked these streets, under his forged name, at the exact moment his rescue was being paid for.

Three — and this one is stranger for being an absence. Mayor Thornwell. Kael'thorne. Any spelling, any name. You searched, Kitty, twice. Nothing. In a city that registers *everyone*, the creature that governed a town up the road for years left no card, no entry, no trace. The register that catches every traveling merchant never once caught the thing wearing a mayor's face.

And on the strange script, Lorien gave you his verdict, the bluntest sage's answer you'll ever get: no language he has ever shelved. "Somebody has *attempted* an elvish code," he said. More attempted than actual. Whoever E. is, they write Elvish like someone who learned it from a great distance — and no scholar in Loftwick can tell you from where.

**[~22:00]**

Alicia. You and Trina climbed to the alcove above Caelith's office, to the Charter Roll and the Marks Volume, chasing the bird-and-coins — and you caught it. The mark belongs to Cindren and Vhal. Bonded factors. A great private banking house of Rel Astra — the kind of house that doesn't lend to shopkeepers; the kind that lends to *governments*. And here is the detail that made the whole room colder: they keep an office in this city. A small satellite notary office. On Wick Lane. One clerk registered: Hesren Vesh.

The same lane where the dead clerk kept his rooms.

And Alicia — before you climbed those stairs, you had already done something else. You opened Iridesca, and you wrote names into her pages to see what the ethereal would give you back. Haskur Vandrell. Moraven. Gilst. Kael'thorne. And then you wrote four more words: "and who is E?" The pages absorbed the ink and gave you nothing — but I want the table to notice what Alicia did there. She put the fey and the banker and the dead man and the initial *on the same page*. Nobody told her to. The same initial that writes threats to a wizard who can't die wrote letters to a creature that wore a man's face for years. Whatever E. is — it corresponds with banks, and it corresponds with *things*.

[don't linger. let it echo.]

And Selvara. You took the Grain Register. The archivist Millicent — steel-trap mind, mustard on her cuff — promised you her supporting papers "first thing tomorrow morning," and pointed you past the leaflets to the person behind the middle name on the fragment: Tamsin Moraven. Senior grain factor, twelve years in the trade, counting house on Tannerway by the eastern caravan yard.

You went. And you found her chief clerk, Doril Veth, red-eyed in the way of a woman who has decided not to cry at work — because Mistress Moraven is *gone*. Walked out mid-work, eleven days ago, "to a customer's farm." People in the middle of something do not do that. The fragment said "delayed." You are now looking at what "delayed" means when it happens to a person.

And then Doril opened the last ledger her employer left, and you found the crown jewel of the entire day. Insurance claims, filed by Cindren and Vhal's Hardby office, on three South Province grain shipments. A storm claim — filed eleven days before the storm. A spoilage claim — filed three weeks before the spoilage. A seizure claim — filed two days before the seizure. Hundreds of thousands of gold, riding on losses that hadn't happened yet. Remember the hummingbird? Watching. Predicting. *Harvests.* Gianni and Kitty theorized it off a feather; Selvara walked into a counting house and found it operating at scale, with a paper trail.

Oh — and the office's carbon copies? Stolen. The day before you arrived. Blank leaves left in their place. Someone got there first. Someone is *getting everywhere* first.

One more thing from that room, because it matters twice. The Duke stands secondary insurer on those shipments. This scheme takes money *out of the Duke's pocket*. The leaflets all over this city accusing him of the grain scandal have the truth exactly upside down — which, as Doril put it, is presumably the point. It's all a lie, and it was planted. Gianni — weeks before anyone proved it, you had already put the Duke on the right side of the equation: someone is using *his* agricultural system to bleed *him*. You called the victim before the evidence did.

And Trina, walking home, mentioned almost in passing that she part-paid the school fees of Irena Moraven — Tamsin's younger daughter — and vouches for the family, with the best argument in the world: conspirators drowning in eastern gold do not borrow tuition money.

**[~27:00]**

So. Let me hand you your case file, detectives — because that is what you are, and I don't think you've noticed.

[slow down here. this is the point of the whole thing.]

You have established — not guessed, *established* — that Haskur Vandrell's deaths were a funded service; Gianni said the word "funding" before anyone showed you a bank. You have established there is a paymaster above him who signs E., who frightened a man that death couldn't hold — "it's my hold on you," Gianni said, and the threat note proved her right. You have established the money is institutional — Selvara called "banking" from cover and texture alone, and Alicia followed the mark until it had a name: Cindren and Vhal, of Rel Astra, with a door on Wick Lane. You have established the scheme — Gianni's own sentence: crop failures, loans, debt, control — and Selvara found it running in the wild, filing claims against the future. You caught the leak in the Hand's own office — Kitty said it to Caelith's face before he could finish confessing it. You caught the inverted lie in the leaflets. You caught a poisoning inside a gaol and read it, correctly, as silence bought at speed. You even caught the absence — a mayor with no paper trail, in a city that files everyone.

Every one of those sentences came out of one of *your* mouths. I have the notes. You are better at this than you think you are — and the people you are hunting agree with me, because look how hard they are working to stay ahead of you. A watcher's poison in the gaol. Stolen carbons in a counting house. They are not treating you like adventurers. They are treating you like a *threat*. And here's the thing about that — every move they've made against you has cost them something. A poisoner spent inside a gaol. A burglar spent inside a counting house. Every time they reach out to stay ahead of you, they leave another fingerprint in another patch of wax, and you have already proven — repeatedly, on the record — that you are exactly the kind of people who find fingerprints. Two months you've been standing at the mouth of that lane, and in all that time I have not been able to think of a single deduction you got *wrong*. Slow down and hear that. Not one.

Two questions are still open on your board, and I'll say them once more, plainly, and then never again tonight. A ledger of names with no numbers on it: what is being counted? And a woman silenced mid-word behind locked doors: how far inside does the reach go — and who told them you were coming?

[pause. then bring the lights down into scene.]

Now put your cloaks back on.

It is nightfall in Loftwick. The lamps are being lit along the Civic Way. Trina has peeled away toward the Little Palace — deliberately, so that no palace shadow falls across what you do next. Selvara — you're across town, closing your interview with Doril Veth, the ledger's ink still in your eyes, Millicent's papers promised for the morning. Cam kept her bed today, and honestly, given how today went, she may have been the wise one.

The rest of you — Alicia, Kitty, Gianni, Elle — are standing together at the mouth of a narrow street in the falling dark. Wick Lane. Three doors ahead of you.

Behind the first, under a placard of a bird above three coins: the satellite office of Cindren and Vhal — one registered clerk, Hesren Vesh, who does not know you're coming.

Behind the second: the Shambord House — the boarding house where a dead man kept his rooms. Where Merro Gilst's entry was closed, his death certified by a physician nobody's questioned, his card stamped with a banker's mark.

Behind the third: a private home, minding its own business. Probably.

The lane is quiet. The kind of quiet that has been *arranged*.

[look up. look at each of them.]

Three doors. One street. Every thread you pulled today — the dead clerk, the banker's mark, the certified death, the watching eyes — every single one of them runs into this lane and stops. You didn't get herded here. You *deduced* your way here, one honest step at a time, and the proof is that the enemy got here first and is pretending not to be home.

You are the Friends of the Common Defense. You are the five people in this city who read its silences. It is evening, on Wick Lane. Somewhere behind you, lamplighters are working their way up the Civic Way, and somewhere ahead of you, behind one of those three doors, is the next piece of the truth. The street is waiting to find out which door you knock on first.

What do you do?
